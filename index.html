Physics Summary & Key Concepts

Nikolova and Zimmerman experimentally demonstrated the detection of the time-dependent electromagnetic 4-vector potential at radio frequencies (1.3 GHz) using biased low-temperature plasma (fluorescent tubes) as detectors. They constructed a wireless link in which the conventional E and H field vectors are ideally zero along the axis of a transmitting dipole, yet a longitudinally polarized magnetic vector potential Az propagates and is measurable.

The key insight is that the RF power observed at the receiver is not radiated RF power from the transmitter. Instead, it results from conversion of local DC bias power in the plasma detector through interaction with the vector potential. This supports the physical reality of the 4-potential in classical electrodynamics, consistent with the Aharonov-Bohm effect at RF.

Core Experimental Findings

  • Longitudinal Az is directly detectable with biased plasma detectors
  • Detector responsivity ~186,000–264,000 m/H (measured vs modeled)
  • Signal falls as 1/r (lossless propagation for potential wave)
  • Phase of potential signal reverses with DC bias polarity (power signal does not)

Important Physical Notes from the Paper

Lossless 1/r falloff: Unlike conventional radio waves in lossy media or conductors (skin effect), the longitudinally polarized potential wave propagates with amplitude falling strictly as 1/r in free space with no dissipation from eddy currents. The paper's model and measurements confirm this behavior over several meters.

Detection via vector potential: The biased plasma interacts with the longitudinal Az through the canonical momentum (mv + eA). The received RF power is generated locally from the detector’s DC bias supply, not from radiated transmitter power — a central result of the experiment.

Modern EW Context:
The simulator models a typical high-sensitivity receiver (NF 1–12 dB, narrowband integration). Real EW systems often use much narrower effective bandwidths via DSP/correlation or longer integration times, dramatically improving SNR for weak potential signals. The received power originates from the detector's local DC bias supply — a key distinction from conventional EM radiation.
